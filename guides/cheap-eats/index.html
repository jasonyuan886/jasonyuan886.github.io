Tea Eggs

茶叶蛋 · chá yè dàn
¥2–3~$0.30

Eggs simmered for hours in soy sauce, black tea, star anise and cinnamon, with marble-like cracks in the shell. The 12-year internet meme foreigners are now trying (and failing) to copy at home — skip the kitchen, they're everywhere in China.

WhereSteel pots at every 7-Eleven, FamilyMart, Lawson — and morning street stalls.
WhenAll day at convenience stores; breakfast stalls 6–9 AM.
来一个茶叶蛋
lái yí gè chá yè dàn
"One tea egg, please."
Grab with tongs, pay at the counter. No cooking, no hunting for star anise — the whole internet is copying China; you can just go.

Jianbing (Chinese Crepe)

煎饼果子 · jiānbing guǒzi
¥8–12~$1.50

Savory mung-bean crepe cooked on a flat griddle, folded around a crispy fried cracker, egg, cilantro and hoisin. Made fresh in front of you in 2 minutes — China's favorite commute breakfast.

WhereStreet carts at subway exits, office areas, hutong entrances.
WhenMorning only, roughly 6:30–9:30 AM. Follow the line of commuters.
一个煎饼，不要辣
yí gè jiānbing, bú yào là
"One jianbing, no spicy."
Wangfujing snack-street versions cost ¥10–15 and are fine, but the cart outside your hotel with the local queue is the same thing cheaper.

Roujiamo ("Chinese Hamburger")

肉夹馍 · ròujiāmó
¥10–15~$1.80

Slow-braised, cumin-spiced pork chopped and stuffed into a toasted flatbread. From Xi'an, 2,000 years old — arguably the world's oldest hamburger, and it beats most actual burgers.

WhereXi'an everywhere; chains and street stalls in most cities. Look for the chopping-board sound.
WhenLunch & dinner, 10 AM–8 PM.
来一个肉夹馍
lái yí gè ròujiāmó
"One roujiamo, please."
In Xi'an, pair it with liangpi (cold skin noodles, ¥10–18) from the same shop — the classic combo, full meal under ¥30.

Baozi (Steamed Buns)

包子 · bāozi
¥1.5–3~$0.30 each

Fluffy steamed buns stuffed with pork, beef, vegetables or red bean. Stacked in bamboo steamers at every morning stall — three of them and a soy milk is a ¥10 breakfast.

WhereAny shop with stacked bamboo steamers out front; chains like 巴比 / 庆丰.
WhenMorning to early afternoon.
两个肉包，一杯豆浆
liǎng gè ròu bāo, yì bēi dòujiāng
"Two pork buns and a soy milk."
Hold up fingers for quantity. Pork (肉包) is the safe default; red bean (豆沙包) is the sweet one.

Xiaolongbao (Soup Dumplings)

小笼包 · xiǎolóngbāo
¥28–58~$4–8 / steamer

Shanghai's delicate dumplings filled with pork and a spoonful of scalding soup. Every tourist's first bite is a mistake — the soup squirts and burns. Eat them the local way.

WhereShanghai (Nanxiang/佳家汤包); dim sum shops nationwide. A steamer = 8–12 pieces.
WhenBreakfast & lunch; locals go before 10 AM.
一笼小笼包
yì lóng xiǎolóngbāo
"One steamer of xiaolongbao."
Bite a small hole in the top, slurp the soup first, dip in vinegar+ginger. Never pop one whole in your mouth.

Stinky Tofu

臭豆腐 · chòu dòufu
¥5–10~$1

Fermented tofu, deep-fried until crispy, drowned in chili-garlic sauce. It smells like a blocked drain and tastes like the best savory snack of your life — the classic China flex food.

WhereNight markets. Changsha's Taiping Street version is the national benchmark.
WhenEvening / night markets after 6 PM.
一份臭豆腐，微辣
yí fèn chòu dòufu, wēi là
"One stinky tofu, mildly spicy."
The smell announces the stall from half a block away — follow your nose, then hold your breath at the first bite.

Tanghulu (Candied Fruit Skewers)

糖葫芦 · tánghúlu
¥5–15~$1–2

Hawthorn berries (or strawberries, grapes, cherry tomatoes) coated in a glassy sugar shell that shatters when you bite. Sweet-sour-crunchy, impossible to eat without smiling.

WhereStreet carts in tourist areas and neighborhood streets alike.
WhenOctober–March — the sugar shell melts in summer. Winter or skip it.
一根草莓糖葫芦
yì gēn cǎoméi tánghúlu
"One strawberry tanghulu."
First-timers: get the strawberry version. The classic hawthorn is mouth-puckeringly sour.

Malatang (Build-Your-Own Spicy Soup)

麻辣烫 · málàtàng
¥20–30~$3–4

A single-person hot pot: grab a bowl, pick skewers of veggies, tofu, meat and noodles from the fridge, hand it over, and it comes back boiled in numbing-spicy broth. You pay by weight.

WhereEverywhere. National chains 杨国福 (Yang Guofu) & 张亮 (Zhang Liang) — thousands of shops; independents are often better.
WhenLunch & dinner, all day.
微辣，谢谢
wēi là, xièxie
"Mildly spicy, thanks."
The default broth is genuinely hot. 微辣 (wēi là, mildly spicy) is the smart foreigner setting — you can always add chili oil.

Hulatang (Henan Pepper Soup)

胡辣汤 · hú là tāng
¥8–12~$1.50

Thick, peppery breakfast soup loaded with beef, tofu skin, vermicelli and mushrooms, eaten with youtiao (fried dough) dipped in. Henan's 36-year-old chains are now opening in London — at London prices.

WhereHenan province breakfast shops (方中山/Fang Zhongshan is the famous chain); elsewhere look for shops with steam clouds and local crowds at dawn.
When6–9 AM only. Follow the steam.
一碗胡辣汤，两根油条
yì wǎn hú là tāng, liǎng gēn yóutiáo
"One hulatang and two youtiao."
In London this bowl costs £13.80 (~¥130). In China: ¥12.
Skip the tourist streets — local breakfast shops open at 6 AM and close by mid-morning.

Hotpot

火锅 · huǒguō
¥80–150~$11–20 / person

The #1 food experience foreigners come for: a boiling pot in the middle of the table, raw plates to cook yourself, chaos and fun. Chongqing/Sichuan spicy broth is the real deal — but order smart.

WhereAny city; chains like 海底捞 (Haidilao, famous for service) or local Chongqing spots. Guijie in Beijing runs to 3 AM.
WhenDinner; go with a group to try more dishes.
要鸳鸯锅，微辣
yào yuānyāng guō, wēi là
"Split pot (half spicy, half plain), mildly spicy."
鸳鸯锅 (yuānyāng guō, "mandarin duck pot") = half spicy / half plain broth. Your safety net. Tripe (毛肚) cooks in 15 seconds — don't overcook it.

蜜雪冰城 · mìxuě bīngchéng
¥4–8~$0.60–1.10

China's viral snowman-branded tea chain, now taking over New York and London. In China the soft-serve cone costs ¥2 and lemon water ¥4 — the same cone is $1.19 in NYC and a comparable drink $3–4. The theme song will be stuck in your head for life.

WhereEvery 200 meters in every Chinese city. Seriously.

The Ordering Survival Kit 🔒

You do not need to speak Chinese. You need a phone, a finger, and six phrases. This section — the phrase cheat sheet, the queue-following rules, and the mobile-payment setup — is part of the full guide.

The universal cheat code

  • Point at what you want — the dish on the next table, the skewer on the cart, the photo on the menu — and say 这个 (zhège), "this one."
  • Hold up fingers for quantity.
  • Vendor types the price on their calculator; you scan their QR to pay.

Finding the good stuff

  • Follow the local queue — a line of locals (especially older ones) means amazing.
  • Avoid stalls with English menus or photos of foreigners — tourist traps, +20–30%.
  • 大众点评 (Dianping) is China's Yelp: look for 4.5+ stars & 1000+ reviews.
  • No tipping, anywhere, ever. Tea is free in most restaurants.

Paying like a local

  • Bind an international bank card in Alipay or WeChat Pay before you land — both now support foreign Visa/Mastercard. Scan the vendor's QR, type the amount, show the "paid" screen.
  • Sit-down restaurants: scan the QR code on the table → Chinese menu opens → use your camera translate / order by photos.
  • Cash still works at some street stalls, but mobile pay is universal in cities.
  • Too tired to go out? Meituan / Ele.me deliver food to your hotel in 15–30 minutes.
